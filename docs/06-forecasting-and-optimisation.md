# F. Forecasting and inventory optimisation

The engine (`engine/src/replen_engine`) implements baselines chosen for explainability. Every formula below maps to a function, and every number that changes a recommendation is written into the calculation trace shown to planners. Accuracy figures quoted here come from synthetic data generated by known processes, so they say the code works, not that it would perform on a real retailer's demand.

## Notation

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| s | demand source: SKU x location x channel (store channel at stores, online channel at the DC) |
| y_t | units sold on day t |
| o_t | observed: item ranged and in stock at open (A-47). Days with o_t = 0 are censored and never used for fitting |
| pi_t | promotion active on day t |
| h | forecast day offset from the planning date (h = 0 is the planning date) |
| alpha | target cycle service level |

## Demand classification

`forecast.classify`, last 365 observed days. With n observed days and k non-zero days:

- fewer than 28 observed days: `new`; no non-zero days: `zero`
- ADI = n / k and CV^2 = (std / mean)^2 of the non-zero sizes (population std)
- smooth (ADI < 1.32, CV^2 < 0.49), erratic (ADI < 1.32), intermittent (CV^2 < 0.49), lumpy otherwise (Syntetos-Boylan cut-offs)

## Item-level pooling and source forecasts

Store-level department-store series are mostly intermittent at daily grain, which hides seasonality. `forecast_sources` therefore forecasts the item's pooled demand and pushes the signal down:

- Share of each source: w_s = r_s / sum(r), where r_s is mean demand on the source's own observed days in the last year.
- Pooled series with censored-source imputation: P_t = sum over in-stock sources of y_st / sum over in-stock sources of w_s. A pooled day counts as observed when in-stock sources cover at least half the demand share.
- Smooth or erratic sources get their own seasonal moving average (own level and weekday profile) with the pooled yearly factors and pooled promo uplift as fallback.
- Intermittent or lumpy sources get a top-down share: F_s(h) = w_s x F_pooled(h).
- Zero sources forecast 0; new sources go to the analogue model.

## Seasonal moving average

`fit_seasonal_ma`:

1. Weekday index (last 364 observed, non-promo days): raw_w = mean_w / mean, shrunk d_w = 1 + (raw_w - 1) x c_w / (c_w + 8), renormalised to mean 1.
2. Promo uplift: for each promo day with at least 5 non-promo observed days within +/-14 days, compare weekday-adjusted demand with that local baseline. raw = sum(promo) / sum(local baselines); shrunk lambda = 1 + (raw - 1) x n / (n + 10), clipped to [1, 5]; requires n >= 5 promo days. Comparing against nearby days limits confounding with seasonality.
3. Deseasonalised demand u_t = y_t / (d_w(t) x lambda^pi_t).
4. Level L = mean of u over the most recent window (28, 56, 91, 182, 364 days or all history) that contains at least 14 observed days.
5. Yearly seasonality is switched on only when history covers 400+ days, at least 300 of the last 364 days are observed, mean u >= 0.25, and the variance of 13 four-week block means exceeds what daily noise explains: CV^2_blocks - phi / (mean u x 28) >= 0.3^2.
6. Yearly factor for day h: Y(h) = mean over k in {1, 2} years of (mean u around the target date k years earlier, +/-7 days, + eps) / (mean u over the level window k years earlier + eps), eps = 0.2 x mean u, clipped to [0.2, 5]. It answers "how much bigger was demand at this time last year than at the time that matches my current level?"
7. Forecast F(h) = L x Y(h) x d_w(h) x lambda^pi_h.

Intermittent series whose yearly test passes (garden furniture in October, Christmas lights) use this model instead of SBA, because SBA averages the season away. On the synthetic data, pooling plus this routing cut the garden parasol's backtest WAPE from 5.0 to 1.7 and the run-level WAPE from 0.41 to 0.36.

## Intermittent demand (SBA)

`fit_sba` on observed days only, alpha_s = 0.1: initial size z = mean non-zero size, interval p = n / k; at each demand z += alpha_s (y - z), p += alpha_s (q - p); daily rate = (1 - alpha_s / 2) z / p.

## New products (analogues)

`analogue.py`. Candidates share the category; distance d = 1.0[subcategory differs] + 0.5[brand differs] + 0.25[colour family differs] + |ln(price_a / price_b)|, similarity exp(-d). The top three candidates with at least 90 observed days in the last year at the same location and channel contribute their own forecasts weighted by similarity. The weights are assumptions to be fitted on historical launches (see evaluation). Items launching after the protection period get no replenishment demand and are flagged `PRE_LAUNCH`, because launch stock is an allocation decision.

## Uncertainty

Each source carries a variance-to-mean ratio phi and a relative level variance v:

- phi: squared residuals of u around a centred 15-day mean over the last 91 days, divided by mean u, floored at 1 (at least Poisson noise). SBA and top-down use var(y) / mean(y) of the source.
- v: Var(level) / level^2. Moving average: phi / (L x n_level). SBA: phi / (rate x n_eff), n_eff = (2 / alpha_s - 1) x p. Top-down: pooled v + 1 / non-zero days (share estimation). Analogue: max(0.25, weighted dispersion of analogue rates), so a new product is never treated as better known than +/-50%.
- Over a window with mean mu_W: Var_s = phi x mu_W + v x mu_W^2 (demand noise plus forecast level error).

## Replenishment policy: periodic review, order-up-to (R, S)

`policy.build_line`.

1. Calendar (`calendar.order_cycle`): order date = first order weekday on or after the planning date; delivery = order date + lead time rolled to the next delivery weekday; the same for the next order. Protection period P = next delivery - planning date. Stock on hand plus this order must last until the next order arrives.
2. mu_P = sum over sources and h < P of F_s(h).
3. sigma^2 = sum_s Var_s + (mu_P / P)^2 x sigma_L^2 (lead-time variability, sigma_L from the supplier).
4. Order-up-to S: if mu_P >= 30, S = ceil(mu_P + z_alpha x sigma); otherwise S is the alpha-quantile of a negative binomial with mean mu_P and variance sigma^2 (Poisson when sigma^2 <= mu_P). Safety stock = S - mu_P.
5. Inventory position IP = max(on hand, 0) - reserved + in transit + counted open orders. Negative on-hand is clamped and flagged (A-41). Open orders overdue by up to 7 days are counted and flagged; beyond that they are excluded (A-42). Damaged and returns-pending stock are excluded and shown in the trace.
6. Net requirement NR = max(0, S - IP).
7. Item constraints, in order, each recorded with before and after values:
   - lifecycle: discontinued, or end of life on or before delivery, gives 0
   - case pack: round up when the remainder is at least 25% of a pack (A-44), otherwise down
   - MOQ (rounded up to a pack multiple): applied if the extra units cover at most 28 days of average demand (A-43); otherwise applied and flagged `MOQ_CONFLICT` as blocking, so a planner must choose
   - capacity: cap at capacity - max(IP, 0), floored to packs; flagged, and blocking if the room is below the MOQ
8. Projection: daily available stock without and with the order (receipts on expected dates, in-transit tomorrow, overdue-but-counted tomorrow). A stockout before the delivery date raises `PROJECTED_STOCKOUT_BEFORE_ARRIVAL`.

### Worked example (from `engine/tests/test_policy.py`)

Ordering daily, lead time 7 days, so P = 8. Forecast 10 per day, phi = 1, sigma_L = 1 day, alpha = 0.95.

| Step | Value |
|---|---|
| mu_P | 8 x 10 = 80 |
| sigma^2 | 1 x 80 + 10^2 x 1^2 = 180, sigma = 13.416 |
| S | ceil(80 + 1.6449 x 13.416) = ceil(102.07) = 103 |
| IP | 30 on hand - 5 reserved + 10 in transit + 20 on order = 55 |
| NR | 103 - 55 = 48 |
| Case pack 12 | 48 / 12 = 4.0 packs, 48 units |

## Order-level optimisation (OR-Tools CP-SAT)

`optimiser.optimise_order` runs only when a constraint couples the lines of one supplier order: minimum order value (MOV) or budget. Variables: packs n_i per line, binary "place order" y, MOQ indicator b_i. Constraints: value >= MOV x y, value <= budget, n_i <= upper_i x y, MOQ either met or zero, upper bound = target + 56 days of demand (top-ups) or capacity. Objective (minimise):

```
sum_i  margin_i x (0.4 x safety_shortfall_i + 1.0 x cycle_shortfall_i)  +  holding_i x excess_i
holding_i = unit_cost_i x 25% / 365 / avg_daily_i     (A-48)
```

Cutting into the safety-stock part of a line costs less than cutting expected demand, so budget cuts spread across lines before any line falls below forecast; MOV top-ups go to the lines where extra stock is cheapest to hold. The solver runs single-threaded with a fixed seed so results are reproducible. This is a heuristic, not a stochastic optimisation: shortage is valued at unit margin (A-49) rather than through the demand distribution.

Optimiser use is deliberately narrow. For most lines the closed form above is the answer and is fully explainable as arithmetic; the solver changes quantities only when a supplier- or budget-level rule forces a trade-off, and every changed line records why (`BUDGET_REDUCED`, `MOV_TOPUP`, `ORDER_DEFERRED`).

## Multi-echelon (designed, not implemented)

The slice plans DC purchase orders with DC demand = store demand + online demand (A-45). That ignores store safety stock and batching, so it understates DC variability and ignores where stock should sit. M3 replaces it with:

- Store replenishment from the DC with the same (R, S) policy per store, producing transfer orders.
- DC targets set by the guaranteed-service model on the supplier, DC, store tree: choose internal service times to minimise total safety stock holding cost subject to end-customer service times; a tree solved by dynamic programming, no general solver needed.
- Validation by simulation (below) against the single-echelon baseline.

## Evaluation methodology

What the slice does: a 28-day holdout per item (`planner._backtest`) using the same pipeline, scored on in-stock days only, aggregated to DC demand; WAPE, bias, and a seasonal naive benchmark (repeat the last observed week). Lost sales are estimated as forecast demand on censored days.

| Metric | Definition | Use |
|---|---|---|
| WAPE | sum abs(F - A) / sum A | Volume-weighted error; headline accuracy |
| Bias | sum (F - A) / sum A | Systematic over- or under-forecast; drives stock or availability |
| Forecast value added | 1 - WAPE(model) / WAPE(naive) | Does the model beat doing nothing clever |
| Pinball loss at P10/P50/P90, interval coverage | standard | Quantile calibration; safety stock depends on it (M1) |
| MASE | MAE / in-sample naive MAE | Comparable across scales, for intermittent series (M1) |

What production needs (M1, before any live order):

1. Rolling-origin backtests: 13 weekly origins, horizons matching each item's protection period, segmented by category, demand class and lifecycle stage. Single-origin results like the slice's are noisy.
2. Inventory replay simulation: run the policy day by day over a historical year with actual demand and actual lead times, recording achieved cycle service level, fill rate, stock, and order count. This tests the decision, not only the forecast, and is the only fair way to compare against the legacy system.
3. Challengers on the same harness: BigQuery ML `ARIMA_PLUS`; a global gradient-boosted model with price, promotion mechanic and depth, calendar events and weather features; hierarchical reconciliation across store, DC and online. A challenger replaces a baseline only if it improves WAPE and bias for two consecutive quarters without degrading simulated service.
4. Analogue weights fitted on past launches: for each historical launch, forecast with analogues as of launch date and score the first 8 weeks.
5. Production monitoring: daily WAPE and bias by category in BigQuery with control limits; forecast-value-added versus planner overrides (are overrides improving the forecast?).

## Known limitations of the baselines

- Days and sources are treated as independent when summing variance. Correlated errors (a warm week affects every store) make the true variance larger; safety stock is likely understated for weather-driven items.
- The 28-day level window lags turning points; yearly factors inherit last year's anomalies (stockouts are censored, other disruptions are not).
- Promotions use a single uplift multiplier per item, with no mechanic, depth, cannibalisation or halo effects. No price elasticity, weather or event features.
- Service is cycle service level, not fill rate; a fill-rate target needs the loss function and is planned for M2.
- The optimiser's shortage cost is margin-based, not derived from the demand distribution.
- On the synthetic dataset the run-level backtest WAPE is 0.36 against 0.51 for the seasonal naive, with bias near zero. Furniture and other very sparse items score WAPE above 1 on a 28-day window, which reflects the metric's instability on a handful of units more than model failure.
